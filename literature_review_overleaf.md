Smart buildings are built environments equipped with integrated sensing, communication, and automation systems that enable coordinated operation of key services such as heating, ventilation, air conditioning, lighting, and safety. Their primary objective is to improve energy efficiency, maintain indoor environmental quality, and support reliable building operation. This functionality is achieved through continuous data acquisition from sensors and the use of control algorithms that adjust system behavior based on observed conditions.

A defining feature of smart buildings is the ability to utilize data for informed decision-making. While some implementations incorporate predictive analytics and advanced optimization, these are not strict requirements. At a minimum, smart buildings employ automation that responds dynamically to changes in occupancy, environmental conditions, and operational demands. Interoperability between subsystems, secure data exchange, and basic coordination mechanisms are essential to avoid fragmented control and inefficiencies.

For this project, a smart building is understood as one that leverages available data and automation to maintain comfort and operational efficiency. Predictive modeling and advanced control strategies represent desirable enhancements but are not mandatory. The focus is on ensuring that the building systems can interact, share information, and adapt to varying conditions without relying solely on manual intervention.

Smart buildings rely on models to describe how indoor conditions evolve under external influences and system actions. The literature distinguishes three main modeling paradigms: white-box, grey-box, and black-box approaches. White-box models are based on physical principles such as heat transfer and mass balance. They provide interpretability and strong extrapolation capabilities but require detailed knowledge of building geometry, materials, and system specifications, which makes them costly to develop and maintain. Grey-box models combine simplified physics with parameter estimation from data, often using lumped thermal networks or state-space formulations. This approach reduces complexity while preserving physical meaning, making it suitable for control-oriented applications. Black-box models, in contrast, rely entirely on data-driven techniques such as neural networks or regression models. They can capture nonlinear dynamics and interactions without explicit physical assumptions but demand large, high-quality datasets and often lack transparency.

These differences have practical implications. White-box models are ideal for simulation and design but less feasible for rapid deployment in existing buildings with incomplete documentation. Grey-box models offer a compromise between accuracy and effort, which explains their popularity in predictive control research. Black-box models are attractive when data is abundant and computational resources are available, yet their limited interpretability and poor generalization under changing conditions remain concerns. These considerations have shaped the evolution of control strategies, which build on these models to manage energy use and comfort. Traditional reactive controllers, such as PID loops (Proportional-Integral-Derivative controllers that adjust system outputs based on error feedback), dominate current practice because they are simple and robust. However, they operate without foresight, adjusting only after deviations occur. This reactive nature often leads to oscillations, inefficient energy use, and lack of coordination among subsystems. Model Predictive Control (MPC), a strategy that predicts future conditions and optimizes actions over a time horizon, addresses these limitations by using a model to anticipate system behavior and optimize control actions while respecting constraints. MPC can incorporate forecasts of weather and occupancy and balance multiple objectives, such as cost and comfort. Despite these advantages, MPC adoption is limited by the effort required to develop accurate models and the computational demands of real-time optimization. Learning-based methods, including reinforcement learning (where control policies are learned through trial-and-error interactions with the environment), aim to reduce reliance on explicit models by adapting policies from data or simulations. While promising, these approaches raise concerns about safety, convergence, and interpretability, especially in safety-critical environments like campus buildings.

Recent research explores methods to reduce the complexity of model development through data-driven identification. Sparse regression approaches, such as the Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy), aim to infer governing equations by selecting a minimal set of terms from a predefined candidate library. These methods produce interpretable models but rely on smooth signals and informative excitation, which can be challenging to obtain in building datasets. Neural network-based techniques extend flexibility by learning system dynamics directly from data. Among these, Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODEs) represent system dynamics as continuous functions and solve them using methods that allow smooth optimization, enabling end-to-end training and compatibility with predictive control frameworks. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) further incorporate physical constraints into the learning process, reducing data requirements and improving generalization. Each approach presents trade-offs: sparse regression methods are sensitive to noise, neural models may overfit, and physics-informed strategies require careful tuning.
Neural ODEs have emerged as a technique for representing dynamic systems in a continuous-time framework while leveraging the expressive power of neural networks. Their differentiable structure facilitates integration with control algorithms and optimization routines, making them suitable for applications where system dynamics must be learned from irregular or incomplete data. Studies highlight their ability to handle nonlinearity and maintain compatibility with gradient-based optimization. However, computational demands and the need for regularization remain active areas of investigation, particularly for large-scale or real-time applications.

At the same time, establishing formal guarantees for Neural ODEs remains difficult because the learned dynamics do not provide explicit, physically derived equations. Classical analysis methods—such as Lyapunov stability analysis (showing that a system’s energy-like measure decreases over time), reachability analysis (computing all states the system can attain under given inputs), and invariant set computation (identifying regions the system cannot leave)—assume an interpretable mathematical structure that Neural ODEs typically lack. As a result, the process of proving properties such as stability or safety through formal verification (techniques that mathematically prove system properties) is less straightforward for Neural ODEs than for conventional ODE models.

Formal verification and synthesis tools provide an alternative perspective on guaranteeing behavior. Approaches such as those implemented in UPPAAL use timed automata—mathematical models that capture system states and timing constraints—to generate control strategies that guarantee compliance with safety and comfort requirements. These methods emphasize correctness and robustness by operating on discrete abstractions of building systems. While discretization limits physical fidelity, formal methods are widely studied for supervisory control in complex environments where safety guarantees are critical. Integration with external simulators and controllers enables practical deployment despite the abstraction, and this line of work complements optimization-based strategies by focusing on guarantees derived from model structure rather than from continuous-time dynamics.
