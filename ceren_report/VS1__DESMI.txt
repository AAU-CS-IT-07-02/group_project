Introduction

1

In today’s rapidly changing industrial environment, manufacturing companies face increasing
complexity driven by dynamic markets, evolving customer requirements, and continuous
technological innovation, making complexity management a significant challenge they must
address (Vogel and Lasch, 2016). This complexity stems from rising customer expectations
for customised solutions, expansion into multiple market segments, ongoing technological
advancements, and intensified global competition (H. ElMaraghy et al., 2013). To address these
challenges effectively, organisations increasingly recognise the strategic importance of robust
Product Portfolio Management as a key mechanism for enhancing operational efficiency and
strengthening competitive positioning (Wan, Martin, and Philip, 2012).
Businesses, therefore, develop a wide range of products with diverse specifications to meet
customer needs. With increasing modularisation of components and the standardisation of
interfaces, it becomes easier to develop multiple product variants efficiently (Gershenson, Prasad,
and Zhang, 2010). However, an excessively broad product portfolio often leads to increased
operational complexity. Products with high similarity struggle to differentiate themselves in the
market, thus reducing potential sales by competing against one another (khan and Huffman,
1998). At the same time, intense market competition compels companies to design and produce
products that enhance performance and quality while simultaneously reducing costs, development
time, and lead times, all while striving for improved innovation outcomes (Sören Ulonska and
Torgeir Welo, 2014).
Product complexity increases substantially with the number of variants, particularly in modern
products that integrate mechanical, electrical, software, and control components across multiple
engineering disciplines. This variety-induced complexity is often the result of insufficient
standardisation, limited process streamlining, and inadequate use of commonality across product
families, ultimately leading to increased costs and a greater operational complexity (H. A.
ElMaraghy and W. H. ElMaraghy, 2013).
DESMI is currently experiencing the same increase in their product portfolio. This has resulted
in the creation of 2,153 new Configure For Manufacturings (CFMs), with each CFM estimated
to cost approximately 10,000 DKK. In total, the development of these new CFMs amounts to
21.5 million DKK. In addition, a forecast for 2026–2030 shows that as many as 6,153 CFMs may
be created in the next five-year period, corresponding to an expected cost of 61.5 million DKK.
This projected increase strongly suggests that DESMI’s product complexity continues to grow.
This project aims to explore how DESMI can enhance decision-making by implementing
visualisation methods in order to gain deep insights into product data.

2.1

DESMI A/S

DESMI was founded in 1834 by Henning Smith and is among the oldest companies in Denmark.
Today, DESMI is a global pump and systems manufacturer headquartered in Nørresundby,
Denmark. The company operates 19 subsidiaries worldwide and serves customers in more than
100 countries. Among these subsidiaries are a foundry in China, an assembly hub in China, and
a facility in the United States. (DESMI, 2025b)
2

2.1. DESMI A/S

Aalborg Universitet

DESMI’s activities are divided into six business segments: Marine, New Green Solutions,
Aquaculture, Defense, Process Industry and Industry & Utility (DESMI, 2025a). They design
and manufacture pumps and integrated systems for a wide range of industrial applications.
The company’s products are tailored to customer requirements and are primarily produced as
Configure-to-Order (CTO) or Engineering-to-Order (ETO) solutions.

2.1.1

Product Overview

DESMI’s product portfolio can be divided into three main categories; Centrifugal Pumps,
ROTAN Pumps, and Systems, which can be seen in Figure 2.2. Approximately 50% of revenue
originates from centrifugal pumps, 15% from ROTAN pumps, and 35% from systems, as shown
in Appendix B.1. Each category comprises numerous configurable variants, which provides
flexibility but also increases internal complexity in managing product data, configurations, and
standardisation across product lines.

Centrifugal Pumps DESMI’s centrifugal pumps are highly configurable, with variations in
nominal diameter, flow rate, head, pressure, and temperature. Application areas include marine
& offshore, aquaculture, utility, and defence & fuel. The centrifugal pump portfolio comprises in
total 18 different series. (DESMI, 2025c)
ROTAN Pumps ROTAN pumps are adaptable to a wide range of applications, from hazardous
substances to food production, and are available in cast iron, carbon steel, or stainless steel.
Key application areas include chocolate & sugar, asphalt & bitumen, paint & ink, and soap &
detergents. The ROTAN portfolio includes in total seven different series. (DESMI, 2025c)
Systems DESMI also supplies integrated systems, including Ballast Water Management
Systems, Helicopter Refuelling Systems, FineFog Firefighting Systems, and OptiSave Energy
Saving Systems. (DESMI, 2025c)

Problem Matrix

The problem matrix is an effective method for analysing and diagnosing organisational problems
within production systems. Applying this framework provided a structured and transparent way
to identify links between departmental activities and the root causes of the challenges, focusing
on systemic issues rather than isolated incidents. (Arlbjørn, Riis, and Johansen, 2006) The

the fundamental causes of problems rather than their immediate symptoms. Cause-Effect Chain
Analysis, which is based on the theory of solving inventive problems methodology, extends this
approach by linking causes and effects in a structured chain to reveal the main sources of system
issues. (Lee, Chechurin, and Lenyashin, 2018) While both Root Cause Analysis and Cause-Effect
Chain Analysis are effective for analysing technical or process-related problems, the problem
matrix offers a more practical framework for projects involving multiple departments.

5.2

Modular vs Integral

This section aims to answer research question one and two from Chapter 3, Initial Problem
Statement "How can a product architecture be analysed?" and "How is the product architecture
utilised in different departments?". Therefore, it will be investigated how product architecture
influences product structure, manufacturing, and evolution, with a particular focus on whether
the architecture follows a modular or integral approach. First, the general principles of modular
and integral product architectures are introduced, highlighting their structural differences and
implications for product design and operations. Next, the product architecture at DESMI
is analysed through a shared component analysis of the NSL pump series to determine the
degree of modularity within the series. Finally, the section explores how the lack of component
standardisation may also reflect a limited overview of product configurations, further reinforcing
the integral nature of the current product architecture. This structured investigation provides a
foundation for understanding the existing challenges and identifying potential areas for improving
product structure and standardisation.

5.2.1

Product Architecture

Product architecture defines how the functional elements of a product are organised and
mapped onto its physical components. Functional elements are considered as the functions a
product delivers, where physical components are what create these functions. Overall, product
architecture is the structure of a product, showing how different parts of a product work
together to deliver the intended functions. Therefore, it impacts the product design, product
manufacturing costs and the product evolution costs. (Rasmus Andersen Postdoc, AAU, 2025)
There are two types of product architecture: integral and modular. These two are presented in
Figure 6.4. The integral architecture has no structured mapping between functions and chunks,
where a change in one function will lead to a change to multiple chunks of the product, in other
words, several components are combined with one physical component. Integral architecture
are few with complex interfaces, but improves the products performance. In the modular
architecture, there is a structured mapping between functions and chunks. There exist many and
well-defined interfaces, where a change in one function will lead to a change in just a few chunks
15

Gruppe B125

5. Initial Problem Analysis

in the product. Each function is assigned to a distinct self-contained module with standardised
interfaces, which can compromise the entire product. (Rasmus Andersen Postdoc, AAU, 2025)

Figure 5.4
Integral VS Modular (Rasmus Andersen Postdoc, AAU, 2025)

The product design is driven by the product architecture based on the required functions of
the product and the functions of the elements in the product. The product architecture is
therefore relevant for designing the product in order to reach the desired functionality. The
manufacturing costs of the product can be influenced by the product architecture in different
ways. The required subsystems for the product manufacturing are structured by the product
architecture, and therefore product architecture influences how efficiently a product can be
manufactured. For instance, standardised components and reuse of components across multiple
products decrease the inventory requirements and simplify the supply chain requirements. The
product architecture also has a high impact on product evolution, as modular products allow
upgrades without requiring upgrades for all the components. This enables developing wide
product families, which is the case for several car models, where different cars are manufactured
based on the same foundation. (Rasmus Andersen Postdoc, AAU, 2025)
Now modular and integral product architecture has been presented. Therefore, it will now be
investigated how the product architecture is at DESMI.

The product architecture at DESMI will be examined through a decomposition of their NSL

is systematically compared to identify the shared components across the different NSL pumps.
This approach provides a clear overview of commonalities and differences, enabling a structured
assessment of the product architecture. The result of the analysis can be seen in Figure 6.5, and
the data behind it can be seen in Appendix B.2, Shared Components.

5.3

Product Structure

Maintaining a comprehensive overview of the product structure within a company is essential. A
clear understanding of the product structure enables identification of how individual components
relate to specific products and functions. It also highlights where potential modifications as
well as changes can be implemented. By not having this overview, important understandings
regarding product design, manufacturing and evolution could be lost. The following subsections
will elaborate on the consequences of lacking such an overview.

5.3.1

Product Portfolio

A product portfolio can be defined as the collection of all the company’s products and services
it offers to its customer to achieve the company’s objectives. For DESMI’s case, a product
portfolio will be the collection of all their pump series and related services offered. A diverse
product portfolio will positively affect a company’s performance matrix like the sales volume, as
a wide variety of products can reach a larger customer segment and market share (Wan, Martin,
and Philip, 2012).

5.3.2

Advantages of having a product portfolio overview

Operationally, portfolio transparency reduces quotation time and engineering workload by
making product configurations and component options systematically accessible, thereby
decreasing dependency on tacit knowledge (Anders Haug, Lars Hvam, and Mortensen, 2012). For
product development, visibility into the portfolio structure reveals standardisation opportunities
by showing which components are used across multiple variants (H. ElMaraghy et al., 2013)

5.3.3

Consequences of lack of an overview

Companies without a clear product overview often face difficulty balancing customisation and
efficiency, without systematic configuration management, organisations struggle to maintain
operational efficiency while meeting diverse customer requirements, which affects profitability and
long-term competitiveness (Anders Haug, Lars Hvam, and Mortensen, 2012). Managing a wide
range of integral products becomes difficult when there is a lack of an overview, standardisation
becomes impossible across products, management loses focus on which products to prioritise
CTO processes become time-consuming. There is also unpredictable demand, elevated spare
parts costs, and wasted resources (Ulrich and Eppinger, 2000).

5.3.6

Utilisation of current product structure

The current utilisation of the product structure at DESMI is primarily centred around the sales
function through QPM. As QPM has been developed as a CPQ tool, its main purpose is to

6.2.1

Product Architecture Principles:
Structures

Modular and Integral

Product architecture describes how a product’s functional elements are mapped onto its physical
components and how these components interact to deliver the intended functionality. Functional
elements represent what the product does, while physical components represent how these
functions are realised. As such, product architecture influences product design, manufacturing
efficiency, and product evolution over time. (Rasmus Andersen Postdoc, AAU, 2025)
Two fundamental types of product architecture are commonly distinguished: integral and
modular architectures. In an integral architecture, functional elements are tightly coupled,
meaning that changes to one function often require changes to multiple components. Such
architectures typically involve fewer but more complex interfaces and can lead to high product
performance, albeit at the cost of increased complexity. In contrast, a modular architecture is
characterised by a structured mapping between functions and components, where each function
is assigned to a distinct module with standardised interfaces. This allows changes to be isolated
to individual modules and supports reuse and standardisation across product variants. (Rasmus
Andersen Postdoc, AAU, 2025)

The Need for a Framework

Luh et al. highlight that companies in competitive markets need robust product platforms that
can handle product variety without exploding R&D efforts and time-to-market. They see the
development of product families and platforms as a central strategy, where products should be
highly differentiated, but share a significant amount of the components. (Luh, Ko, and Ma,
2011)
In the same spirit, Ulonska and Welo argue that ETO-businesses often have products and
knowledge which are diffuse and primarily tacit. Without a general overview, the companies
will end up using resources for (re)designing variants instead of improving existing solutions.
Ulonska and Welo therefore call for visual, explicit models that can unify scattered portfolio
information. (Sören Ulonska and Torgeir Welo, 2014)
Mortensen et al. show that the same problem arises in many product configuration projects;
configuration systems are developed without a stringent approach for modelling which results
in unstructured systems that are hard to maintain. They conclude that there is a need for a
modelling method which both describes the product family, the rules which the configurations
are built upon, as well as them being possible to translate into an IT-model. (A. Haug, Hvam,
and N.H.Mortensen, 2010)
These challenges correspond to DESMI’s situation and acts as a justification of why the literature
review focuses on frameworks that address structure, complexity and configuration data.

8.2.2

Design Structure Matrix

AlGeddawy provides a clear description of the classical Design Structure Matrix as a method for
structuring and visualising dependencies between components in a product architecture. In a
DSM, components are placed on the diagonal, and their pairwise relationships are documented in
the corresponding cells, typically using binary values. This makes it possible to identify tightly
coupled components, clusters and potential modules, as well as areas of high interdependency
within a system. (AlGeddawy, 2014)
The article highlights that traditional DSM approaches are primarily suited for analysing single
product structures, since the matrix captures one set of component interactions at a time.
This means that while DSM is effective for revealing local complexity, modularity opportunities
and architectural patterns in a single configuration, it offers limited support for understanding
variation across a wider product family. The authors therefore propose extensions for handling
families, which implicitly shows that classical DSM does not natively represent mutually exclusive
components, variant-specific structures or configuration rules. (AlGeddawy, 2014)
Despite these limitations, the core DSM concept remains a valuable analytical tool because it
provides a structured and transparent way of exploring component relationships before more
formal information models are developed. DSM is particularly suited for identifying complexity
drivers and tightly coupled areas that influence how product structures should be organised.

8.2.4

Product Portfolio Mapping & Knowledge-Based Development

Product Portfolio Map
Ulonska and Welo introduce the Product Portfolio Map (PPM) as a visual tool for mapping and
structuring product variants in an ETO company. They describe how existing delivered products
are analysed and variants mapped in a combined diagram with multiple layers: principal layout,
architectural variance, functional variance, and physical variance. This allows users to see how
functions are realised through physical solutions and where many or few variants exist. (Sören
Ulonska and Torgeir Welo, 2014)
The PPM article also has a literature review of existing modelling methods. It shows, that for
instance Product Family Master Plan, PVM, DSM and generic BOM each capture a certain
aspect of the product modelling, but none of them are able to show variance across multiple
delivered products and abstraction levels in one unified visualisation. PPM is therefore proposed
as a method to map the existing portfolio and make tacit knowledge visual and explicit. (Sören
Ulonska and Torgeir Welo, 2014)

The artefact cannot represent component relations, dependencies, compatibility rules, or details

high-level categorisation and a systematic approach for creating an initial overview, the artefact
itself cannot be used in the subsequent technical development of the system.
PVM scores highly on most of the system requirements, as its structure is based on categories,
attributes, and configuration rules that can be translated into filtering and user-oriented views

PVM can express compatibility only if the underlying relations are known and validated. DSM
replaces CRC cards, which are otherwise used in PVM approaches, since CRC cards assume
simple and predefined relations. DSM, in contrast, can systematically handle DESMI’s complex

or underlying design decisions. (Vice President, PDI, DESMI, 2025) Existing documentation
is dispersed across different systems, files, and personal recollections, resulting in a fragmented
knowledge base. No unified mapping of product variants is available, and the organisation
lacks a structured overview that captures architectural, functional, or physical aspects of its
products. The only structure DESMI currently has is QPM, which is limited, as it only displays
pumps that match preselected criteria. This situation creates a high dependency on individual
expertise, increasing the risk of knowledge loss, inconsistencies and a reduction in organisational
transparency. Consequently, onboarding and design processes become time-consuming and
inefficient due to the absence of a clear and consolidated product structure. In essence, DESMI
operates with tacit, fragmented, and experience-based product knowledge.
However, despite the experience-based and tacit product knowledge, DESMI possesses the
underlying data required to understand the relationships and dependencies within its product
range. This indicates that DESMI has the necessary foundations to develop a comprehensive
product mapping and thereby reduce its reliance on tacit knowledge; the challenge lies in the
fact that such an overview has not yet been established. Several factors may contribute to this
situation, including limited competencies in connecting and structuring relevant data, a lack of
awareness regarding the existing data foundation, or insufficient time and resources to undertake
such an initiative. Nevertheless, the available data suggests that DESMI has the potential to
create a unified and structured overview of its products using its current information base.

Implications of Gap Analysis

The following section summarises research related to the implications identified through the
gap analysis. These studies provide insights into knowledge management, product variety,
and modularity, illustrating how different organisational practices influence efficiency, decisionmaking, and engineering workload. Collectively, they offer a theoretical foundation for
understanding the challenges observed within DESMI’s operations. The following paragraphs
correspond to the implications identified in the Gap Analysis.
Lack of Explicit Documentation
Tacit knowledge creates operational vulnerabilities compared to explicit knowledge. Explicit
knowledge enables clearer mapping of data interrelationships through codification. Tacit
knowledge remains personal, context-dependent, and difficult to transmit across organisational
boundaries. As a result, DESMI may face additional difficulties when onboarding new staff and
experience operational disruptions if key personnel become unavailable. (Sanchez, 2004).
Absence of Multi-Level Product Structure
Without structured architectural decomposition, firms cannot effectively analyse variants or
make informed decisions. Structured decomposition into modules or subsystems is essential for
managing variety complexity (Vladimir and Zuzana, 2023; Monetti and Maffei, 2023; Grønvald
et al., 2025). The absence of such structure, may lead to unclear cost and complexity along the
value chain, making it difficult to allocate indirect costs correctly and evaluate arrangements at
variant level.(Nørgaard et al., 2024)
Fragmented Data Environment
Dispersed documentation creates substantial maintenance challenges. Fragmentation forces
excessive search effort when updating information, leading to outdated or unused documentation.
(Aa et al., 2017).

Lack of Product Portfolio Structure
Without formal portfolio representations, organisations depend on tacit knowledge, slowing operations and increasing turnover risk. This dependency slows quotation processes Mortensenp roductp orf olio.Overl
ElM araghyEtal., 2013).
No Basis for Identifying Standard Solutions
Lacking reusable solutions increases engineering workload. Without stable platforms, engineers
must create bespoke designs requiring greater time and resources (Nørgaard et al., 2025;
Martinez, Leeuw, and Haefliger, 2024). Research also shows that accumulating unique
components without standardisation, creates unnecessary variation and further adding to
engineering effort (Matschewsky and Kambanou, 2025). Conversely, standard modules reduce
duplication and simplify variant opportunities (Oh et al., 2019).
High Dependency on Tacit Expertise
Product variety generates operational complexity, particularly when data structures are
inconsistent. This fragmentation forces employees to create personal tracking systems, increasing
knowledge dependency and reducing efficiency. Standardising product data within enabling
systems is essential for organisational transparency (Sundet et al., 2021).

Missing Variant Transparency
Without clear variant documentation, organisations struggle to understand system boundaries
and product evolution. Lacking organised component information prevents understanding

design changes, resulting to inconsistencies across design and manufacturing (Linsbauer, LopezHerrejon, and Egyed, 2017; M. Hanna and D.Krause, 2020).

9.2.3

Product Focus in the Implementation

Now that the work packages have been defined and a network diagram has been created, both
the activities involved in the implementation and their expected duration are known. However,
a clear point of focus has not yet been established. Within this project, only a single pump,
the NSL300-415, has been mapped, as undertaking an entire pump series was considered too
extensive for the scope of the study. For DESMI, beginning the full mapping process without a
defined starting point would likewise be overwhelming and potentially counterproductive.
The Pareto Chart in Figure 6.2 in Section 5.1, Product Portfolio Identification highlights that
five pump series out of a total of 26 account for more than 80% of overall revenue, representing
approximately 20% of the product range. These series are NSL, DSL, HD, ESL, and GP. Focusing
on these five series would enable DESMI to initiate the mapping effort with the products that
deliver the greatest strategic impact. Furthermore, should DESMI seek a quicker and highly
visible effect, prioritising the mapping of the NSL series would be a particularly impactful starting
point, due to its large contribution.
Given this prioritisation, it becomes important for DESMI to monitor and manage the progress
of the mapping effort in a structured manner. Establishing a KPI to track the proportion of
products that have been mapped would provide clear visibility of progress and ensure that the
implementation remains aligned with strategic objectives. In the context of this project, only
the NSL300–415 has been mapped so far, representing roughly 1% of the total product portfolio
when considering its revenue contribution and the number of components involved. To ensure
that such a KPI is meaningful and operationally useful, DESMI should determine how each
pump or pump series contributes to the overall portfolio in percentage terms. This will clarify
the weighting of each product within the KPI and ensure that progress is measured in a way
that accurately reflects the relative importance and impact of different pumps and pump series
within the organisation.

9.3.1

Tool Selection for Dynamic Mapping

Although the framework in Figure 8.4 outlines how DESMI can structure and map its products,
the project ultimately presents only a static example, as illustrated in Figure 9.7. This visual
demonstration is valuable for showing how the structure might appear, yet it offers limited
practical use on its own. For DESMI, the most beneficial outcome would be the implementation
of a tool capable of supporting a fully dynamic mapping process.
The dynamic nature of such a tool is twofold. Firstly, it would allow users to actively interact
with the mapping, ensuring that the full product overview can be accessed and explored rather
than being restricted to a single, fixed representation. An example of an interactable database is
created and available in Appendix B.9, Mapping data. The database contains all possible options
for the ten different NSL300-415 pumps. Secondly, a dynamic tool would enable continuous data
input, allowing DESMI to update and expand the system as new products, configurations, or
relationships are introduced. Once new mappings are created and linked to existing structures,
the tool would automatically incorporate and display these changes. This ensures that the
mapping remains current and that knowledge can be contributed across the organisation rather
than being limited to isolated individuals.
In terms of selecting a suitable tool, Microsoft Power BI presents a strong candidate. DESMI
already makes substantial use of Power BI for reporting and other forms of dynamic analysis,
meaning there is existing in-house expertise that would reduce training costs and implementation
time. Power BI also includes functionality for building decomposition trees, offering a structure
closely aligned with the example shown in Figure 9.7. Its ability to ingest and refresh data
further supports the requirement for an adaptable, constantly evolving mapping environment.
Nevertheless, Power BI is only one possible solution. DESMI should evaluate alternative tools
and engage relevant stakeholders to ensure that the final choice aligns with practical needs,
technical capabilities, and long-term strategic considerations.

9.3.2

Analytical Opportunities

Life Cycle Evaluation and Lead Time Insights
With a complete and structured overview of products and their configurations, DESMI gains
the ability to conduct life cycle analyses that support more strategic portfolio planning. By
applying dynamic mapping tools, factors and weightings can be assigned to products, media
types, and components, allowing detailed comparisons across configurations, for instance, among
the ten versions of the NSL300-415. Such analysis makes it possible to understand how life cycles
differ, when certain configurations should be phased out, and whether new alternatives should
be introduced. This could be done by using a PLM system. Incorporating lead time data further

enhances this insight, enabling the identification of configurations with the longest or shortest
delivery times, and highlighting which components contribute the most to these variations. This
combined perspective can guide decisions on which configurations to prioritise, modify, or remove,
while offering a deeper understanding of supply-chain influences.

Cross-Series Comparison and Portfolio Alignment
The system also enables DESMI to analyse product offerings within a single series or across
multiple series. For example, with a full overview of the NSL series, it becomes possible to

decisions on whether a new pump should be added to a series, whether an existing pump no
longer fits the strategic direction, or whether broader restructuring is wanted. If demand grows
for a particular media type and the overview reveals that no NSL pump supports it, this would
indicate a clear need for a new addition. Similarly, when comparing across series, the system
can highlight overlaps that suggest consolidation or completely new opportunities where a fresh
series might be necessary. This analytical capability strengthens the alignment between product
offerings and actual market needs.

Identification of Low-Value or Redundant Components
With the complete overview integrated with sales data, DESMI can more easily identify
components, configurations, or entire pumps with minimal or no market activity. Items that
have been sold only once, components that are rarely used, or pumps with consistently low
demand become clearly visible through the system’s structure. Recognising these low-value or
redundant elements enables DESMI to make informed decisions about whether certain items
should be removed from the portfolio, thereby reducing unnecessary complexity. To ensure that
such decisions are consistent and well-justified, a structured decision tree could be established,
guiding each potential removal through defined evaluation steps before a final conclusion is
reached. This, in turn, helps minimise manufacturing and inventory costs and refine the overall
component landscape. Such insight plays a key role in keeping the product range focused,
competitive, and aligned with customer requirements.

Potential Impact on Future Item Creation and Complexity
A comprehensive mapping of DESMI’s product portfolio could contribute to a reduction in
the number of new items created, and consequently a decrease in annual CFMs. With clearer
visibility of existing variants, it would become easier to determine whether a new variant is
necessary or whether an existing option meets the requirements. Should such an overview prove
effective, the forecast of CFMs for 2026–2030 in Figure 6.13 could be revised downward. This
outcome would not only reduce costs associated with working hours but also help to lower overall
organisational and product complexity.

This chapter seeks to discuss the effect of the chosen limitations and the assumptions applied
throughout the project.

10.1

Reconsidering the Degree of Modularity

The suggestion that the product architecture adopts an integral structure appears less certain
upon closer examination. The conclusion is based solely on the NSL series, where many
components identified in the shared component analysis fit only a single pump. This finding
may be less definitive than initially suggested. Some components may be compatible with future
NSL variants not yet developed, though this accounts for only a minor part of the uncertainty.
The analysis also excludes the remaining product series, and had these been included, a more
modular architecture might have emerged, as components could potentially span multiple series.
Consequently, it cannot be stated with full confidence that DESMI follows an integral product
architecture.

10.2

Reflections on the Proposed Portfolio Mapping
Framework

The proposed framework for mapping the product portfolio, inspired by PPM and adapted
through the inclusion of ER diagrams, DSM, and PVM, appears suitable for the intended
purpose. Nonetheless, an existing, more established framework may have provided a closer
fit. Replacing certain PPM steps may also have excluded elements that could have contributed
valuably to the process. In addition, the framework may be constrained by the DSM, particularly
given the manual effort required to construct it. Exploring options for generating the DSM
dynamically could have reduced the workload. However, DESMI may already possess more
efficient, data-driven methods for producing such a model, informed by its internal knowledge
and system structures.

10.3

Uncertainties Related to Work Packages

The estimated durations of the work packages remain broad and may not accurately represent
the effort required for implementation. This introduces the risk of the actual cost exceeding the
proposed estimate. In addition, the indicated hourly cost is not fully certain and may fluctuate,
further affecting the overall budget. The planning also assumes that DESMI will prioritise the
project, with departments such as product management being available to complete their tasks as
scheduled in the network diagram presented in Figure 9.9. The extent to which each department
can allocate time is unknown, and limited availability could extend the implementation timeline
considerably. The uncertainty surrounding the time estimates also affects the validity of the
88

10.4. Assumptions and Limitations within the New Item Creation process Aalborg Universitet
network diagram itself. If the durations of activities change, the critical path may shift, altering
both the sequence of tasks and the total implementation time. Such changes would require the
development of a revised network diagram and a recalculated critical path, potentially leading
to a different overall project duration. Furthermore, the best and worst case scenarios for hours
and cost are based on a simple doubling, which may not reflect realistic variation. As a result,
the true spread between the two scenarios remains uncertain.

10.4

Assumptions and Limitations within the New
Item Creation process

The project assumes that reducing the time spent on creating a CFM within the NIC process
will lead to an equivalent reduction in cost. A 50% decrease in time, for instance, is presumed
to yield a 50% decrease in cost. This proportional relationship may not be realistic. Time
requirements could initially rise, as employees would need training to use the new tool, and
the learning period may temporarily increase workload rather than reduce it. In addition, the
NIC process illustrated in Figure 6.10 is based on the stakeholders identified, yet the process
itself could have been examined in greater depth. For example, the specific steps that consume
the most time were not investigated. With clearer insight into these time-intensive activities
and more accurate time estimates, it would have been possible to determine the solution’s true
impact on the NIC process with greater confidence.

10.5

Considerations Regarding the Proposed Solution

The solution has been developed on top of the existing QPM structure to maintain a general
and broadly applicable approach. However, the absence of a department-specific focus may
have resulted in the omission of important factors, categories, or functional requirements. A
more targeted design, centred on the needs of a particular department, might have produced a
more effective and potentially different outcome. The initial applicability of the solution is also
uncertain. Certain elements required by specific departments may be missing from the current
framework. If a department chooses to adopt the solution, substantial time may be needed
to identify missing factors and subsequently establish the necessary relationships to integrate
these into the structure. This additional work would require both time and financial resources,
potentially delaying the realisation of benefits.

10.6

Influences on the Net Present Value Calculation

Several factors contribute to uncertainty in the NPV result. The assumption of a directly
proportional relationship between time reduction in the NIC process and the associated cost
savings has a significant effect. If this relationship does not hold, and the savings realised
by DESMI are lower than anticipated, the resulting NPV would be less favourable than the
calculated value. Reassessment would therefore be appropriate once actual savings are known.
In addition, the implementation cost derived from the work packages and the network diagram
may lack precision. Should the true cost prove higher, this would further reduce the NPV.
Consequently, both the benefit and cost assumptions introduce risk into the accuracy of the
financial evaluation.

Conclusion

11

This project set out to investigate how product data and structural relationships can be organised
to improve transparency, standardisation, and decision-making within a complex industrial
product portfolio. Through an in-depth analysis of a pump series within DESMI’s centrifugal
pump portfolio, the study demonstrates that while the organisation possesses a high degree of
product flexibility, this flexibility is currently supported by fragmented data structures, tacit
knowledge, and inconsistent representations of how products, components, and variants are
connected. These conditions limit reuse, hinder cross-functional collaboration, and constrain the
organisation’s ability to develop and maintain a shared understanding of its product complexity.
The analysis of the existing product structure revealed an integral architecture, characterised
by low component reuse and a high number of unique parts. While such a structure supports
customisation, it also contributes to increased complexity and reduces the organisation’s ability
to manage variation efficiently. This challenge is reinforced by the way product data is currently
utilised, where different departments rely on disconnected tools, informal communication, and
individual expertise rather than a unified and explicit product overview. As a result, duplicated
work, inconsistent information, and limited organisational visibility emerge as systemic challenges
rather than isolated operational issues.
In response to these findings, the project proposes and applies a combined framework based on
DSM and PVM principles. Rather than focusing solely on product architecture, the framework
establishes a structural foundation that makes relationships between components, configurations,
and variants visible and traceable. The execution of the framework demonstrates that fragmented
information can be consolidated into a coherent product overview that supports analysis of
variants, dependencies, and standardisation potential, as well as portfolio-level decision-making.
At the same time, the implementation relies on several assumptions regarding data availability,
effort estimation, and organisational prioritisation. The time and cost estimates associated with
implementation remain uncertain, and deviations in resource availability or task duration could
affect both the realisation timeline and the projected benefits.
Beyond its structural contribution, the solution addresses several organisational implications
associated with a limited product overview. By externalising knowledge that was previously
tacit, it reduces dependency on individual experts and lowers vulnerability to personnel changes.
However, the initial adoption of the framework may require additional effort, particularly as
departments familiarise themselves with the structure and identify missing elements relevant to
their specific needs. During this learning period, short-term workload may increase rather than
decrease, and the anticipated efficiency gains may only materialise over time. Furthermore, the
framework has been developed to remain broadly applicable across the organisation, which may

limit its immediate effectiveness for department-specific use cases without further adaptation.
From an economic perspective, increased transparency enables more informed discussions about
variant rationalisation, reuse potential, and system boundaries, supporting both short-term
efficiency improvements and long-term portfolio development. While the economic assessment
indicates that the solution has the potential to deliver tangible value through reduced engineering
effort, shorter lead times, and lower configuration complexity, the financial results are sensitive
to underlying assumptions. In particular, the assumed proportional relationship between time
savings in the New Item Creation process and cost reduction introduces uncertainty. Should
actual savings differ from expectations, the resulting net present value would need to be
reassessed.
Overall, the project demonstrates that establishing a clear and explicit product overview is a
critical enabler for managing complexity in highly configurable product portfolios. Product
architecture analysis plays an important role in revealing structural characteristics, such as the
integral nature of the portfolio, but the primary contribution lies in making complexity visible and
understandable across the organisation. While the proposed approach is subject to uncertainties
related to implementation effort, organisational adoption, and financial assumptions, it
nonetheless provides a robust and scalable foundation for future analysis. By enabling a shared
and transparent understanding of products, components, and variants, the framework supports
informed decision-making and creates a platform for continuous improvement across R&D, sales,
product management, and potentially other departments at DESMI, aligning with the company’s
CTO ambitions.

Businesses experience a growing demand for different product variants, which results in an
increasingly complex product portfolio. (Vogel and Lasch, 2016) This calls for a higher degree
of modularisation as well as a shorter time-to-market for products. As this becomes more
pronounced, it is increasingly important for businesses to have a full overview of the products,
components and possible configurations.
For managing a larger project, it is recommended to follow a structured and systematic approach.
In this project, the SDC was used to minimise the risk of overlooking critical steps or essential
information. The SDC itself is a generic framework within engineering and IT projects, which
enables its use in a wide variety of contexts.
The literature did not reveal any framework able to make a full overview, from the foundational
data structure to the end visualisation of the products and their components. Using a
hybrid model of different artefacts, such as the DSM, ER, and PVM can create more
comprehensive approach to product architecture analysis than stand-alone methods. This
integration demonstrates a wider methodological potential beyond the boundaries of DESMI’s
case. Where individual artefacts tend to focus on only one aspect of the product structure, the
hybrid approach points towards a future where mapping, structuring and maintaining product
knowledge increasingly relies on blended models that capture both functional dependencies and
configurational variety.
For other CTO and ETO businesses, the solution can contribute to gaining an overview of their
products and options within them. This could streamline the configuration and engineering
processes and lead to shorter lead times and lower costs of creating new variants. The product
overview prevents CTO and ETO businesses from beginning from scratch every time. Having
an overview of the products also enables different analyses that can lead to beneficial decisions,
including lower complexity, decreasing inventory and phasing out products.
The project therefore contributes to a broader academic discussion on how model-based methods
can be combined to support system development in areas with a high degree of variety and
interdependency. The approach also contributes to the discussion by opening up the possibility
for further exploration. The manual creation of the models and the reliance of knowledge of
domain experts point to opportunities for more automated approaches for mapping.

The Funnel Model is applied to structure the report in a systematic and coherent manner.

A.2.2

Problem Matrix

The problem matrix is an analytical tool used to identify and organise issues that occur within and
between departments of a company. By placing departments along the columns and categorising
problems as "inherited, internal, and forwarded" along the rows, it enables to visualise how
difficulties originate and move through the organisation. (Arlbjørn, Riis, and Johansen, 2006)

helping to identify connected issues and the causes that affect one another. This visual structure
helps to highlight how one department’s challenges may influence the performance of another.
In the context of this project, the problem matrix was particularly relevant, as the departments
involved in the process are in constant interaction and frequently exchange information. Such
interdependencies can lead to situations where incomplete or inaccurate data passed from one
department to another results in increased workload, overconsumption of resources, or delays in
processes.

Requirements serve as specifications for what the system must accomplish, establishing targets
for designers to achieve (Nicholas and Steyn, 2021). Comprehensive high-level requirements
encompass the system’s objectives (the overarching aims that elaborate on identified needs),
life cycle considerations (production, operation, maintenance, and disposal), operational modes
(different scenarios and environments in which the system functions), constraints (policies,
standards, available resources, time, and budget limitations), and interfaces (interactions with
other systems in the environment). These requirements must address the diverse needs of
all stakeholders such as producers, operators, users, and maintainers reflecting their different
perspectives: management focuses on costs and capacity, operators prioritize performance and
reliability, while end-users emphasize usability and safety.
Developing a system concept involves iterative cycles of top-down decomposition (breaking
the system into smaller components), bottom-up synthesis (integrating components into larger
assemblies), and evaluation (verifying that results satisfy requirements) (Nicholas and Steyn,
2021). This process occurs within the SDC, which guides projects through prescribed
developmental stages. System definition begins with defining user needs and requirements to
achieve a clear understanding of what the end-item must accomplish.
A critical distinction exists between user requirements and system requirements (Nicholas and
Steyn, 2021).
User requirements originate from the user’s perspective and articulate needs in general terms,
such as desired performance levels or functional outcomes. These are gathered directly from
users who will operate or benefit from the system. In contrast, system requirements represent the
contractor’s or developer’s perspective, translating user needs into solution-oriented specifications
that define the approach, architecture, and functions necessary to satisfy user requirements.
System requirements also incorporate developer objectives beyond user needs, such as resource
utilization and profitability considerations. This translation from user to system requirements
ensures a common understanding among the development team regarding project deliverables
while maintaining alignment with user needs.

A.2.4

Gap Analysis

The gap analysis is a process which commonly plays an important role for organisations in
identifying where they currently stand in comparison with where they aim to be. The strategy
essentially involves comparing current performance, procedures, or capabilities with a clearly
defined future state, allowing managers to spot gaps that might otherwise remain unrecognised.
This method can be carried out in four steps: (1) analysing the shortcomings of the current
situation, (2) defining the desired or future state, (3) identifying the gaps between these two states
and outlining their implications, and (4) implementing the necessary changes and organisational
strategies to address the problems. (Gupta, 2020; Kim and Ji, 2018)
The gap analysis is explained to allow companies to identify inefficiencies, skill shortages, and
inconsistencies, making it easier to prioritize actions and allocate resources. It is applicable
across disciplines and industries, making it an accessible approach that can deliver clear and
timely results. It is also noted that gap analysis ensures no critical issues are overlooked when
designing improvements. (Kim and Ji, 2018; Gupta, 2020) Moreover, it has been shown that it is
valuable in strategic initiatives such as digital transformation, as it helps companies plan the steps
needed to reach long-term goals. (Shabdin et al., 2024) Together, these findings demonstrate

that gap analysis is a practical tool for improving performance while also supporting informed
decision-making in industry.

A.2.7

Entity Relation Diagram

An ER diagram is used for planning a database. It illustrates different tables and how they
are connected with primary keys and foreign keys. This enhances the understanding of how the
database is structured and the understanding of complex database structures. An illustration
of the database helps align all members’ understanding of the database by providing a bird’seye view of the database. The ER diagram simplifies the representation of the database, and
databases become more manageable. This also helps identify data redundancy and data gaps in
a database. For modelling the database, the logical ER model is preferred over the conceptual
ER model because the logical ER model includes primary keys and foreign keys. This provides
a better understanding of how the tables in the database are linked to each other. The physical
ER model provides a concrete plan for setting up and implementing the database. This requires
an in-depth solution for a database and locks it to one specific system. Therefore, the logical ER
model is preferred in this project. (Belcic and Stryker, 2025)

A.2.8

Design Structure Matrix

The DSM is a matrix that analyses and visualises the dependencies between components, people,
activities, etc. The matrix consists of the same elements in the first column and first row
and visualises which elements are dependent on each other. For example, in Figure A.5, it
is visualised that Element A is dependent on Elements B and F. The columns present which
elements depend on a specific element, and the rows present which elements a specific element
depends on. (Eppinger and Browning, 2012)
